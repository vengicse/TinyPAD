# 1. Introduction

Wearable Internet of Things (IoT) devices—wristbands, chest straps and patch sensors—now make it possible to monitor physiological signals continuously and outside the clinic. Stress, which has been identified as a cause of physical and psychological harm when prolonged [@gedam2021; @taskasaplidis2024], and cardiac arrhythmias, which call for real-time electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring for timely intervention [@an2024], are two prominent health anomalies that wearables can detect early. Detecting them on the device itself, rather than streaming raw signals to the cloud, avoids exposing sensitive data, reduces latency and saves the radio energy that dominates the budget of battery-powered sensors [@ponnada2024; @kim2023tinyml; @choi2025ecg]. Tiny machine learning (TinyML) and edge AI have therefore become central to smart-healthcare research, with deployments ranging from stress classifiers on microcontrollers [@abusamah2025] to arrhythmia classifiers running on ARM Cortex-M4 wearables [@kim2022] and dedicated low-energy ECG processors [@zhang2025cop; @liu2025jssc].

Four obstacles still separate laboratory accuracy from dependable continuous monitoring. First, *inter-person variability*: physiological baselines differ strongly between people, so models trained on one population degrade on a new wearer; reviews of stress detection emphasise personalised models [@bolpagni2024], and inter-patient variation is a recognised cause of accuracy loss for arrhythmia classifiers [@liu2025jssc; @zakaria2024]. Second, *multimodality with unreliable sensors*: combining electrodermal activity, photoplethysmography, ECG, respiration, temperature and motion improves detection [@bolpagni2024; @xiang2025; @li2023pilot], but real wearables suffer from noise and from sensors that drop out [@rashid2023]. Third, *resource limits*: models must fit kilobytes of memory and run in milliseconds on microcontrollers [@abusamah2025; @kim2022]. Fourth, *energy*: continuous sampling and inference of every sensor drains the battery, motivating event-driven and hybrid processing [@nazari2025; @yoo2025; @choi2025ecg].

This study addresses these obstacles jointly and pursues three research objectives:

**Objective 1 (personalised multimodal detection).** To detect health anomalies—stress episodes from multimodal chest and wrist signals and abnormal heartbeats from two-lead Holter ECG—with high sensitivity and specificity on *previously unseen* people, adapting to each person using only a short, label-free resting calibration.

**Objective 2 (TinyML-ready, robust edge inference).** To keep the detector small enough for microcontroller-class wearables (tens of thousands of parameters, int8 weights, millisecond latency) and robust to missing sensors.

**Objective 3 (energy-aware monitoring).** To reduce the sensing and computation needed for continuous monitoring through a causal cascade that keeps power-hungry sensors and the full model off unless low-power signals suggest an anomaly, while preserving detection quality.

To meet these objectives we propose **TinyPAD** (*Tiny Personalised multimodal Anomaly Detector*). TinyPAD encodes every sensor modality with a tiny depthwise-separable 1-D CNN, fuses the modality embeddings with a masked attention mechanism trained with random modality dropout, and personalises the detector to each wearer without labels by expressing every input signal relative to the wearer's own resting baseline, recorded during a five-minute calibration at rest. An always-on stage-1 TinyPAD that uses only low-power wrist signals (or RR intervals for ECG) gates the full multimodal stage-2 model and the duty-cycled sensors. The main contributions are:

1. **A personalised, label-free multimodal TinyML detector.** TinyPAD adapts to a new wearer from five minutes of unlabelled rest data, without fine-tuning and without anomaly labels, by expressing all modalities (and, for ECG, the RR rhythm) relative to the wearer's own resting baseline. We also evaluated two further label-free adaptation mechanisms—re-estimating BatchNorm statistics on the rest segment (AdaBN [@li2018adabn]) and stacking an embedding-deviation score—and report that, on validation people, both reduced detection quality; they are therefore not part of the final design.
2. **Missing-sensor-robust fusion for wearables.** Masked modality-attention fusion trained with modality dropout lets one model operate with any subset of sensors, which is required both for sensor dropout in practice and for the energy cascade.
3. **A causal, energy-aware sensing cascade.** A low-power stage-1 model with a recall-calibrated threshold decides when to wake the duty-cycled sensors and the stage-2 model. The sensor-gating rule is causal: sensors switch on only after a flag and must cover a full window before it is classified. The resulting sensor on-time and computation savings are reported against the accuracy they cost.
4. **A rigorous comparison on two public wearable datasets.** On WESAD (leave-one-subject-out) and the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database (inter-patient DS1→DS2 split) we compare TinyPAD with a hand-crafted-feature random forest, a convolutional auto-encoder, early- and late-fusion CNNs, a CNN-LSTM and a tiny transformer under identical inputs, training budgets and validation protocol, and we report ablations, missing-sensor robustness, calibration-length sensitivity, int8 quantisation, latency and model size.

**Paper organisation.** The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews wearable stress and arrhythmia detection, TinyML and energy-efficient edge processing, personalisation and multimodal fusion, and closes by stating what the present study takes from, and adds to, the state of the art. Section 3 presents the proposed methodology, the working model of TinyPAD (Fig. 1) and its mathematical formulation. Section 4 describes the datasets, features and experimental protocol and reports and discusses the results through eight figures and five comparative tables together with ablation and sensitivity studies. Section 5 concludes and outlines future enhancements, followed by the code and data availability and authorship statements.

# 2. Related Work

**Wearable stress detection.** Reviews of wearable stress detection catalogue ECG, EEG, PPG, EDA, respiration and temperature sensors and the machine-learning pipelines built on them, and highlight continuous monitoring as the key benefit of wearables [@gedam2021; @chen2021; @taskasaplidis2024; @pinge2024]. Heart-rate-variability features derived from ECG have been shown to be useful stress markers [@dalmeida2021]. On WESAD, SELF-CARE uses context-aware selective fusion of wrist or chest sensors to cope with noise [@rashid2023]; a Siamese late-fusion model classifies four affective states from wrist BVP and EDA [@choi2025]; and a 1-D CNN trained on WESAD has been transferred to activity recognition [@almadhor2025]. Other work fuses time- and frequency-domain features of accelerometer, EDA, heart-rate and temperature signals [@xiang2025] or combines a CNN with a transformer over ECG, EMG, EDA, respiration and temperature for pilot stress [@li2023pilot]. A scoping review of *personalised* stress detection concludes that EDA and PPG are the most frequently used biosignals, that deep models are gaining ground, and that computationally efficient, user-centric models are still needed [@bolpagni2024]. Reported accuracies vary widely with the evaluation protocol, and several of the reviewed studies do not evaluate on held-out subjects.

**Arrhythmia detection under the inter-patient paradigm.** For beat classification on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database [@moody2001], the inter-patient paradigm of de Chazal et al. [@dechazal2004] separates training and test patients to obtain unbiased estimates. Under this paradigm, improved residual CNNs [@li2022], Fourier-decomposition features with feature selection [@fatimah2024], two-lead ensemble learning [@zakaria2024] and capsule/sequence-to-sequence networks for class imbalance [@zhou2023] have been proposed; supraventricular beats remain the hardest class [@li2022; @fatimah2024]. Personalisation has been explored through personalised federated learning with feature alignment [@tang2021].

**TinyML and energy-efficient edge health monitoring.** TinyML deployments include an XGBoost stress model fitted into the 2 MB of a Raspberry Pi Pico RP2040 [@abusamah2025], a NASNet-XGBoost model for multi-disease prediction [@ponnada2024], a CNN-based ECG classifier on an Arduino prototype [@kim2023tinyml], a lightweight CNN on an nRF52840 Cortex-M4 [@kim2022], and a knowledge-distilled arrhythmia model on an STM32 wearable [@an2024]. Hardware-oriented work reaches nanojoule-level energy per classification with dedicated CNN coprocessors [@zhang2025cop], two-stage ultra-low-parameter networks with power-of-two quantisation [@zhang2024ecgp], binarised depthwise-separable CNNs [@zhang2024bdscnn] and an arrhythmia processor that combines heartbeat-difference classification, event-driven computation and adaptive neural-network wake-up [@liu2025jssc]. At the system level, near-data pre-diagnosis that transmits features instead of raw ECG extends battery life [@choi2025ecg]; GRU models with PSO-tuned hyper-parameters reduce energy and latency [@liu2025tce]; a hybrid design runs a TinyML binary detector on the wearable and forwards anomalies to a server [@nazari2025]; and an edge-AI framework combines federated learning, blockchain and homomorphic encryption for secure patient monitoring [@prabha2025]. Closest to our work, Yoo et al. developed a self-personalised, signal-processing anomaly detector that updates its decision criteria from each patient's recent ECG and activates a Siamese network only for abnormal beats [@yoo2025].

**Contributions from the state of the art to the current study.** The reviewed works provide the foundation and the gap for TinyPAD. From the TinyML and hardware literature [@abusamah2025; @kim2022; @an2024; @zhang2024bdscnn; @zhang2024ecgp] we adopt depthwise-separable, quantisation-friendly convolutional encoders and report parameters, memory and latency. From the event-driven and hybrid designs [@nazari2025; @yoo2025; @liu2025jssc; @zhang2024ecgp] we adopt the principle that a cheap first stage should wake an expensive second stage only when needed. From personalised stress and ECG research [@bolpagni2024; @tang2021; @yoo2025] we adopt the use of the wearer's own normal data, and from multimodal stress research [@rashid2023; @xiang2025; @li2023pilot] the fusion of complementary biosignals and the need for robustness to noisy sensors. From the inter-patient ECG literature [@dechazal2004; @li2022; @fatimah2024] we adopt evaluation on unseen people. The present study adds what these works do not provide together: (i) a single multimodal detector that is personalised to a new wearer *without labels and without fine-tuning* through per-person baseline normalisation of every modality, together with an empirical comparison against AdaBN- and deviation-score-based personalisation; (ii) attention fusion that tolerates any subset of missing sensors and therefore also serves as the low-power stage of a cascade; (iii) a *causal* sensing cascade whose savings are measured on sensor on-time as well as computation; and (iv) a controlled comparison against six baselines on two public wearable datasets with leave-subject-out and inter-patient protocols. In our searches of OpenAlex (2021–2025; queries on TinyML health, wearable and multimodal stress detection, WESAD, inter-patient and lightweight arrhythmia classification, personalised and energy-efficient wearable ECG, missing-modality physiological learning) we did not find an earlier method that combines these elements; the closest designs [@yoo2025; @nazari2025; @liu2025jssc] address single-modality ECG, and studies we could read only at abstract level may contain related ideas.

# 3. Proposed Methodology

## 3.1 Problem formulation

A wearer $u$ carries a body-area network of $M$ sensor modalities. The signal stream is segmented into windows (30-s windows with a 5-s stride for multimodal stress monitoring; one heartbeat for ECG), and window $i$ is a set of multichannel signals $x_i=\{x_i^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{C_m\times L_m}\}_{m=1}^{M}$ with label $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ ($1$ = health anomaly: stress episode or abnormal beat). Training data come from a set of people $\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{tr}}$; the detector is evaluated on people $u\notin\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, for whom only an unlabelled calibration segment $\mathcal{C}_u$ (the first five minutes, recorded at rest where the protocol provides a rest period) is available before monitoring starts. The design problem is
$$
\max_{f}\ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{te}}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{te}}}\ \mathcal{Q}\big(\{f(x_i;\mathcal{C}_u),y_i\}_{i\in u}\big)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad |f|\le P_{\max},\ \ \mathrm{MAC}(f)\le B_{\mathrm{MAC}},\ \ \mathbb{E}[E_{\mathrm{sense}}+E_{\mathrm{comp}}]\le E_{\max}, \tag{1}
$$
where $f(\cdot;\mathcal{C}_u)$ is a detector personalised with the calibration segment of person $u$, $\mathcal{Q}$ is a detection-quality measure (F1, AUC, sensitivity/specificity), $|f|$ is the number of parameters bounded by the device memory $P_{\max}$, $\mathrm{MAC}(f)$ is the number of multiply–accumulate operations per window bounded by the compute budget $B_{\mathrm{MAC}}$, and $E_{\mathrm{sense}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{comp}}$ are the sensing and computation energy per window, bounded by $E_{\max}$. Eq. (1) maps the three objectives of the study onto quality on unseen people (Objective 1) under memory, compute (Objective 2) and energy (Objective 3) constraints.

## 3.2 Working model of TinyPAD

Fig. 1 shows the working model. The wearable network is split into *always-on* low-power modalities (wrist accelerometer, wrist EDA and skin temperature for stress; RR-interval features, which require only an R-peak detector, for ECG) and *duty-cycled* modalities (wrist BVP, chest ECG, respiration, EDA/temperature and accelerometer; the ECG beat morphology of both leads). Before monitoring starts, a five-minute unlabelled **resting calibration** (①) provides the wearer's personal baseline statistics (per-channel means and standard deviations, and the mean resting RR interval), which both stages use. The **stage-1 TinyPAD** (②) runs continuously on the always-on modalities and produces a personalised anomaly score. When this score exceeds a threshold calibrated on validation people for high recall, the **sensor-gating** block (③) switches the duty-cycled sensors on for a hold period; because the decision is made at the end of the flagged window, a later window can be analysed by stage 2 only if the sensors were on for its whole duration, which makes the cascade causal and physically realisable.

The **stage-2 TinyPAD** (④) processes all modalities. Each modality is first normalised with the wearer's own resting statistics, then encoded by a tiny depthwise-separable 1-D CNN (an MLP for the RR feature vector) into a 32-dimensional embedding. A masked modality-attention module weights the embeddings of the available sensors and sums them into a fused representation; because the network is trained with random modality dropout, the same weights remain valid when sensors are missing. A small classifier head turns the fused representation into a logit margin, whose sign gives the decision that drives the **real-time alert** (⑤). Windows that stage 2 does not analyse raise no alert, i.e. they are treated as normal (Eq. 6). The only person-specific information is the resting baseline, which requires no anomaly labels from the new wearer.

## 3.3 Mathematical formulation

**Personal normalisation.** For person $u$ with calibration windows $\mathcal{C}_u$, every channel $c$ of every signal modality $m$ is standardised with the person's resting mean and standard deviation, followed by a global standardisation fitted on the training people,
$$
\tilde{x}^{(m)}_{c}=\frac{1}{s_c^{(m)}}\left(\frac{x^{(m)}_{c}-\mu^{(m)}_{u,c}}{\sigma^{(m)}_{u,c}}-\bar m_c^{(m)}\right),\qquad
\mu^{(m)}_{u,c}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_u|L_m}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_u}\sum_{l=1}^{L_m}x^{(m)}_{i,c,l}, \tag{2}
$$
where $\sigma^{(m)}_{u,c}$ is the corresponding standard deviation, $L_m$ the window length of modality $m$, and $\bar m_c^{(m)}$, $s_c^{(m)}$ the mean and standard deviation of the person-normalised training data. For the ECG rhythm modality the four RR-interval features are instead divided by the wearer's mean resting RR interval, which removes the individual heart-rate level. Eq. (2) converts absolute physiological levels, which differ strongly between people, into deviations from the person's own baseline.

**Masked modality-attention fusion.** Let $e_m=E_m(\tilde{x}^{(m)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ be the embedding produced by encoder $E_m$ and $\kappa_m\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether modality $m$ is available (during training $\kappa_m$ is sampled with dropout probability $p_{\mathrm{md}}$). The fused representation is
$$
z=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\alpha_m e_m,\qquad \alpha_m=\frac{\kappa_m\exp\big(w^\top\tanh(W e_m+b)\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\kappa_j\exp\big(w^\top\tanh(W e_j+b)\big)}, \tag{3}
$$
where $W\in\mathbb{R}^{16\times D}$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$ and $w\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$ are learnable attention parameters. Missing sensors receive zero weight and the remaining weights renormalise, so the detector degrades gracefully instead of receiving corrupted inputs.

**Training objective.** The encoders, attention module and head $h$ are trained on the training people with the class-weighted cross-entropy
$$
\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\omega_{y_i}\log\frac{\exp\big(h_{y_i}(z_i)\big)}{\sum_{c\in\{0,1\}}\exp\big(h_c(z_i)\big)},\qquad \omega_0=1,\ \ \omega_1=\frac{N_0}{N_1}, \tag{4}
$$
where $N_0$ and $N_1$ are the numbers of normal and anomalous training windows; the weighting compensates for the rarity of anomalies (abnormal beats and stress windows are the minority classes).

**Decision rule and personal calibration.** At inference the anomaly score of window $x$ for wearer $u$ is the logit margin of the network applied to the person-normalised input,
$$
s_u(x)=h_1\big(z(\tilde x_u)\big)-h_0\big(z(\tilde x_u)\big),\qquad \hat y=\mathbb{1}\big[s_u(x)>0\big], \tag{5}
$$
where $\tilde x_u$ denotes the input normalised with the statistics of person $u$ (Eq. 2). Personalisation therefore enters only through the resting baseline, which is computed once from the unlabelled calibration windows $\mathcal{C}_u$ and costs one mean and one variance per channel on the device. Two richer label-free mechanisms were also evaluated on validation people (Section 4.8): AdaBN, which replaces BatchNorm running statistics by those of $\mathcal{C}_u$ [@li2018adabn], and a stacked score $a\,s_u(x)+b\,d_u(x)+c$ with the diagonal Mahalanobis deviation $d_u(x)=\log\big(1+\tfrac{1}{D}\sum_j (z_j-\hat\mu_{u,j})^2/\hat\sigma^2_{u,j}\big)$ of the fused embedding from its calibration moments $\hat\mu_u,\hat\sigma^2_u$.

**Causal energy-aware cascade.** Let $s^{(1)}_u$ be the score of the stage-1 TinyPAD trained on the always-on modalities, and $\tau$ the threshold that retains a fraction $\rho$ of the anomalous validation windows ($\tau$ is the $(1-\rho)$ quantile of their stage-1 scores). A flag at window $j$ (ending at time $t_j+T_w$) switches the duty-cycled sensors on for a hold period $T_h$, and stage 2 is run on window $i$ only if the union of the hold intervals opened by earlier flags covers it completely:
$$
g_i=\mathbb{1}\Big[\,[t_i,\,t_i+T_w]\subseteq\bigcup_{j:\ s^{(1)}_u(x_j)\ge\tau}\big[t_j+T_w,\ t_j+T_w+T_h\big]\Big] \qquad\qquad (6a)
$$
$$
\hat s_u(x_i)=\begin{cases}s_u(x_i), & g_i=1\\ \min\big(s^{(1)}_u(x_i),\,s_{\min}\big), & g_i=0\end{cases} \qquad\qquad (6b)
$$
where $t_i$ is the start time of window $i$, $T_w$ the window length and $s_{\min}$ a constant below every stage-2 score, so that windows not analysed by stage 2 are treated as normal. Because each flag is raised only at the end of its window, a window can never wake the sensors for itself. For ECG, where the front end is always on and only computation is saved, stage 2 is run directly on every beat whose stage-1 score reaches $\tau$ ($g_i=\mathbb{1}[s^{(1)}_u(x_i)\ge\tau]$). The expected energy per window is then
$$
\mathbb{E}[E]=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{A}}P_m T_w+\eta\,\mathrm{MAC}_1+\bar g\Big(\sum_{m\in\mathcal{D}}P_m T_w\Big)\phi+\bar g\,\eta\,\mathrm{MAC}_2, \tag{7}
$$
where $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ are the always-on and duty-cycled modalities, $P_m$ the power of sensor $m$, $\eta$ the energy per multiply–accumulate of the processor, $\mathrm{MAC}_1$ and $\mathrm{MAC}_2$ the operations of the two stages, $\bar g$ the fraction of windows processed by stage 2 and $\phi\ge1$ the ratio between the sensor on-time and the stage-2 coverage (the sensors stay on during the hold period). Because $P_m$ and $\eta$ are device-specific, we report the two measurable factors of Eq. (7)—the relative computation $(\mathrm{MAC}_1+\bar g\,\mathrm{MAC}_2)/\mathrm{MAC}_2$ and the duty-cycled sensor on-time fraction $\bar g\phi$—instead of absolute joules.

**Algorithm summary.** For each new wearer: (a) record five minutes at rest and compute the personal normalisation statistics (Eq. 2); (b) in every window, run stage 1 on the always-on sensors and update the sensor-gating state; (c) when the duty-cycled sensors cover the window, run stage 2 (Eqs. 3 and 5); otherwise treat the window as normal (Eq. 6); (d) raise an alert if the final score is positive.
