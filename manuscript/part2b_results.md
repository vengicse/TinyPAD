## 4.4 Detection performance on unseen people

Tables 3 and 4 compare TinyPAD with the six baselines, and Fig. 3 shows the per-subject distribution on WESAD together with the DS2 results on MIT-BIH. On **WESAD**, TinyPAD obtains the highest accuracy ({{wesad_t_acc}}), stress F1 ({{wesad_t_f1}}), sensitivity ({{wesad_t_sens}}) and balanced accuracy ({{wesad_t_bacc}}) of all methods. Its F1 exceeds that of the strongest baseline, {{wesad_bb}} ({{wesad_b_f1}}), by {{wesad_gain_f1}} points. TinyPAD has the higher F1 on {{wesad_wins_bb}} of the 15 unseen subjects, but the subject-level difference to {{wesad_bb}} is not statistically significant (Wilcoxon p = {{wesad_p_bb}}), whereas the differences to RF-HCF (p = {{wesad_p_rf}}, {{wesad_wins_rf}}/15 subjects) and to the CAE, EF-CNN, CNN-LSTM and TinyTransformer (p ≤ {{wesad_p_max_sig}}) are significant at the 5 % level; after Holm correction for the six comparisons, the difference to RF-HCF is no longer significant (p = {{wesad_p_rf_holm}}), whereas those to the CAE, EF-CNN, CNN-LSTM and TinyTransformer remain so (p ≤ {{wesad_p_holm_max4}}). TinyPAD is not the best method on every metric: the random forest on hand-crafted features has a higher specificity ({{wesad_rf_spec}} versus {{wesad_t_spec}}), and LF-CNN and RF-HCF reach a higher subject-averaged AUC ({{wesad_lf_auc}} and {{wesad_rf_auc}} versus {{wesad_t_auc}}). This means that these baselines rank the windows *within* a subject well but place their fixed decision threshold less well on unseen people (LF-CNN: sensitivity {{wesad_lf_sens}}, specificity {{wesad_lf_spec}}), whereas TinyPAD's person-relative inputs keep the natural threshold meaningful for a new wearer and give the best balance of sensitivity and specificity. When the scores of all subjects are pooled (Fig. 4), RF-HCF attains the highest pooled AUC ({{wesad_pool_rf}}), followed by TinyPAD ({{wesad_pool_t}}) and LF-CNN ({{wesad_pool_lf}}).

On **MIT-BIH**, TinyPAD achieves the highest sensitivity ({{mitbih_t_sens}}), balanced accuracy ({{mitbih_t_bacc}}) and AUC ({{mitbih_t_auc}}). ## 4.5 Edge efficiency

Table 5 and Fig. 5 relate detection quality to model cost. On WESAD the TinyPAD stage-2 model has {{wesad_params}} parameters ({{wesad_kb8}} kB with int8 weights) and requires {{wesad_macs}} MACs per 30-s window; single-window inference takes {{wesad_ms}} ms on one CPU thread. The stage-1 model that runs continuously needs only {{wesad_s1_macs}} MACs. On MIT-BIH the model has {{mitbih_params}} parameters ({{mitbih_kb8}} kB int8) and {{mitbih_macs}} MACs per beat, and the always-on RR stage needs only {{mitbih_s1_macs}} MACs. TinyPAD is not the cheapest network—the early-fusion models and the TinyTransformer down-sample all signals jointly and need fewer MACs—but it is the only model that combines the highest F1 on WESAD with a memory footprint far below the limits of current wearable microcontrollers. Because the per-window computation is fixed and small, real-time operation is limited by the window rate (one decision every 5 s for stress, one per heartbeat for ECG), not by inference time.

## 4.6 Robustness to missing sensors

Wearable sensors frequently lose contact or power. Table 6 and Fig. 6 remove one modality at test time. TinyPAD's masked attention redistributes the fusion weights over the remaining sensors, whereas the EF- and LF-CNN receive zero-filled inputs. Because the EF- and LF-CNN were not trained with modality dropout, this comparison shows the benefit of the combination of masking and modality-dropout training rather than of the attention module alone; the ablation (Table 9) shows that a masked mean fusion trained the same way performs similarly. On WESAD, TinyPAD keeps an F1 of {{wesad_miss_t_mean}} on average over the seven single-sensor dropouts (worst case {{wesad_miss_t_worst}}, without chest respiration), compared with {{wesad_miss_lf_mean}} for LF-CNN and {{wesad_miss_ef_mean}} for EF-CNN. On MIT-BIH, TinyPAD is the best of the three models when lead 1 is missing; when lead 2 is missing, EF-CNN is marginally better ({{mitbih_miss_ef_lead2}} versus {{mitbih_miss_t_lead2}}). Losing the RR-rhythm modality degrades every model strongly (TinyPAD {{mitbih_miss_t_rhythm}}), which confirms that rhythm information is indispensable for arrhythmia detection. Removing the first lead even raises TinyPAD's F1 ({{mitbih_miss_t_lead1}}), which we attribute to the attention then relying more on the rhythm cue, which generalises well to unseen patients.

## 4.7 Energy-aware cascade

Table 7 and Fig. 7 evaluate the causal cascade over a range of stage-1 recall targets ρ. On **WESAD**, keeping the duty-cycled chest sensors and wrist PPG off unless the wrist-only stage 1 flags a window reduces their on-time to {{wesad_c98_on}} of the monitored time at ρ = 0.98. Stage 2 then analyses {{wesad_c98_rate}} of the windows, and the overall computation falls to {{wesad_c98_comp}} of always-on operation. The price is a lower F1 ({{wesad_c98_f1}} versus {{wesad_full_f1}} for always-on TinyPAD), mainly through lower sensitivity: the causal rule cannot analyse the first window of a stress episode with the full sensor set, because the sensors are switched on only after a flag. The cascade nevertheless clearly outperforms stage 1 alone (F1 {{wesad_st1_f1}}). On **MIT-BIH** the ECG front end must stay on to obtain RR intervals, so the cascade saves computation rather than sensing. At ρ = 0.90 only {{mitbih_c90_rate}} of the beats reach stage 2 ({{mitbih_c90_comp}} relative computation), and F1 *increases* to {{mitbih_c90_f1}} (always-on: {{mitbih_full_f1}}) because the rhythm stage filters out normal beats that the morphology model would have flagged; sensitivity drops to {{mitbih_c90_sens}}. Between these extremes, ρ is a single knob that trades energy, sensitivity and specificity, and an operator can set it according to the clinical cost of missed versus false alarms.

## 4.8 Ablation and design selection

Table 8 documents how the final design was chosen using validation people only. Among the personalisation options, person-wise normalisation of the inputs alone gave the best validation F1 on both datasets ({{dev_w_logit}} on WESAD, {{dev_m_logit}} on MIT-BIH). Adding AdaBN or stacking an embedding-deviation score reduced it (e.g. {{dev_w_adabn}} and {{dev_w_stack}} on WESAD), so these mechanisms were rejected. For fusion and modality dropout, attention fusion was best on both datasets, with $p_{\mathrm{md}}=0.25$ on WESAD and $p_{\mathrm{md}}=0.10$ on MIT-BIH.

Table 9 and Fig. 8 report the same variants on the evaluation seeds. Personal normalisation is the single most important component: removing it lowers F1 from {{abl_w_full}} to {{abl_w_nopn}} on WESAD and from {{abl_m_full}} to {{abl_m_nopn}} on MIT-BIH. Modality dropout is also beneficial on WESAD ({{abl_w_nomd}} without it). On the evaluation seeds, two simpler variants score higher than the final design: mean fusion on WESAD ({{abl_w_noatt}}) and no modality dropout on MIT-BIH ({{abl_m_nomd}}). These variants were not preferred by the validation-only selection, and we did not change the design after seeing test results. The discrepancy shows that the differences between these fusion variants are within the variance of a 15-subject and a 22-record test set. Modality dropout remains necessary for the missing-sensor robustness (Section 4.6) and for the stage-1 model of the cascade. The rejected AdaBN and deviation-stacking variants are also worse on the evaluation seeds on WESAD ({{abl_w_adabn}} and {{abl_w_dev}}); on MIT-BIH, AdaBN gives a slightly higher F1 ({{abl_m_adabn}}) but a lower AUC and balanced accuracy than the final design.

## 4.9 Calibration length and quantisation

Table 10 and Fig. 9 show that personalisation needs little data. With 30 s of rest data the WESAD F1 is already {{cal_w_05}} and rises to {{cal_w_2}} with 2 min; longer calibration brings no further gain ({{cal_w_5}} with the full 5 min, which is the setting used in all other experiments), so about two minutes of rest data are sufficient. MIT-BIH is almost insensitive to the calibration length ({{cal_m_05}} with 0.5 min, {{cal_m_5}} with 5 min), because the RR normalisation only needs the mean resting interval. Post-training int8 quantisation of the weights changes the F1 by less than one point ({{q_w_fp}} → {{q_w_i8}} on WESAD, {{q_m_fp}} → {{q_m_i8}} on MIT-BIH) while reducing the weight memory four-fold to {{q_w_kb}} kB and {{q_m_kb}} kB.

## 4.10 Discussion and threats to validity

The results show that the decisive factor for detecting health anomalies in *unseen* wearers is a person-relative input representation. A five-minute, label-free rest calibration lets a 9–24 k-parameter network outperform larger baselines on WESAD and detect far more supraventricular beats on MIT-BIH, without fine-tuning on the device. Masked attention with modality dropout adds graceful degradation under sensor loss, and the causal cascade turns the multimodal detector into an energy-scalable system. More sophisticated label-free adaptation (AdaBN, deviation scoring) was harmful in our validation study, which cautions against assuming that test-time adaptation always helps on small physiological cohorts.

Several threats to validity apply. *Construct validity:* WESAD stress is induced by a laboratory protocol, and the normal class (baseline plus amusement) does not cover daily-life activities; MIT-BIH contains 47 patients recorded decades ago with a Holter device. *Internal validity:* all methods share the same data, folds, training budget and early-stopping rule, but only TinyPAD uses the calibration segment, by design. A baseline given the same personal normalisation corresponds to the "concatenation fusion" variant in Table 9, and its F1 is close to TinyPAD's, so much of the gain should be attributed to personalisation rather than to the fusion architecture. Hyper-parameters of the baselines were not tuned individually beyond early stopping. The WESAD cascade numbers were recomputed after we corrected the denominator of the sensor on-time (initially the full recording span instead of the monitored time); the corrected values are reported. This re-run retrained the three WESAD TinyPAD seeds with the same code and seeds on a different GPU; because GPU training is not bit-wise deterministic, the WESAD TinyPAD scores differ slightly from the first run, and all WESAD TinyPAD numbers in this paper (Tables 3, 5–7, 9 and 10) are taken from the re-run. *Statistical conclusion validity:* on WESAD the F1 advantage over LF-CNN is not significant at the subject level (that over RF-HCF is significant without correction, p = {{wesad_p_rf}}, but not after Holm correction for the six comparisons, p = {{wesad_p_rf_holm}}), and on MIT-BIH, after Holm correction, TinyPAD's F1 is significantly higher only than that of the CAE (the uncorrected advantage over LF-CNN, p = {{mitbih_p_lf}}, becomes p = {{mitbih_p_lf_holm}}); the MIT-BIH t-tests over seeds use one fixed test set and therefore capture training variability, not sampling variability over patients; the advantages in sensitivity, balanced accuracy and S-beat detection are larger. *External validity:* energy is reported through sensor on-time and MACs rather than measured joules, and latency was measured on a server CPU rather than on a microcontroller.
